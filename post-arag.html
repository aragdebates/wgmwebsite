Aanya's Story✳By Aanya Raghavan✳

Title consolation prize.

On the difference between being welcomed into a room and being expected to make it comfortable for everyone else.

I used to think the most important question in a debate room was whether I had made the best argument. Then I started noticing how often the question underneath was different: had I made the room comfortable enough for other people to hear me?

There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from being one of the only women or gender minorities on a team. It is not just the work of debating. It is the quiet calculation before speaking, the decision about whether to correct someone who has called you “shrill,” the extra energy spent making your confidence palatable. Sometimes the debate is about the resolution. Sometimes the debate is about whether you are allowed to take up the space your argument requires.

We deserve to be challenged on our ideas without being asked to survive the room first.

The part that rarely gets called strategy

Sexism in debate does not always arrive as a dramatic incident. Often, it looks like a pattern. A male teammate gets described as “assertive” while a girl making the same point is “difficult.” A judge reads directness as aggression. A younger debater learns to apologize before asking a question. Every individual moment can look too small to name, but together they shape who gets to feel at home in the activity.

For a long time, I treated community care like something separate from debate: a nice thing, important but secondary, to do after the real work was finished. I do not believe that anymore. If the real work is building a space where people can think, risk, learn, and stay, then care is part of the infrastructure. It is not a consolation prize for people who could not win the round.

What care can look like

It can look like a mentor texting after a hard tournament and asking a specific question instead of offering a generic “you got this.” It can look like a team naming expectations around interruptions before the first practice. It can look like believing someone when they tell you a comment landed badly, even if the speaker did not mean harm. It can look like making sure the person who is always taking notes gets to lead the conversation too.

None of these choices makes debate less rigorous. They make it more honest. They give more people a chance to spend their energy on the argument instead of on proving that they deserve to be there.
