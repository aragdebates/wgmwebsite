Making space is a practice, not a mood.

What changes when inclusion becomes something we build into pairings, feedback, leadership, and every ordinary day.

“We want everyone to feel included” is a beautiful sentence. It is also not a plan. I have heard it in team meetings, at camps, and in the soft landing after someone has named a problem. The sentence matters. But it only becomes real when it changes what happens next.

In debate, exclusion can be built into the ordinary. Who gets the first choice of partner. Whose prep is treated as the team’s baseline. Who receives patient feedback, and who gets labeled as behind. Who is asked to take on care work because they are “good with people,” then passed over when leadership becomes visible.

Inclusion is the set of repeated choices that tells someone: your presence was planned for.

Designing better defaults

We can change some of this by designing better defaults. Rotate who facilitates. Make feedback specific and actionable. Ask what kind of support someone wants before giving advice. Build a clear process for reporting harm that does not depend on the person affected becoming a perfect narrator of their own pain.

These are not dramatic gestures. That is part of their power. They create a culture that does not rely on one exceptionally kind captain or one brave person speaking up at exactly the right time. They make care less fragile.

From invitation to ownership

I am interested in the difference between inviting someone into a space and giving them ownership of it. An invitation says: you may enter. Ownership says: your ideas can change the structure. A collective that wants women and gender minorities to stay in debate has to do more than make us visible. It has to give us meaningful influence over what debate becomes.

That is why I believe in mentorship, chapters, and storytelling at the same time. A mentor can help one person find a next step. A chapter can change the default for a whole team. A story can give language to someone who thought they were the only one. Together, those choices turn “you belong here” from a compliment into a condition we keep building.
